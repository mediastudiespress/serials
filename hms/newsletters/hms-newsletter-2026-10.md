1. Anderson-Horne, LaRisa. "We should all be talking about religion*: Religion, Black studies, and the epistemic grounds of media study." _Social Media + Society_ 12, no. 3 (2026): 20563051261467822. <https://doi.org/10.1177/20563051261467822>.
> *ABSTRACT:* This essay interrogates the nature of critical religious study in the field of media and communication. Religion is understudied as a critical quality of the human experience in our field, from journals to academic positions in communication departments to specialized grant funding to conferences and more. Recently, White Christian nationalism has swept the academy and blown a small tailwind of interest in “religion” as a demographical marker or political identity. While these are helpful in some instances, these depictions are often reductive, unjustly universalized, and Western-centric. Instead, I detail the epistemological contributions of Black studies of religion and Black media studies to argue that we need to be studying religion more rigorously. After briefly reviewing the contributions of J. Kameron Carter’s Anarchy of Black Religion and Armond Towns’ On Black Media Philosophy, I explain how religious study is vital to any analysis of power and human flourishing. What we need now more than ever are studies of religion that seriously engage what we mean by “religion” and “media” in ways that demand liberatory action.

2. Baldacchino, Karl. "Jean Baudrillard and the disappearance of resistance: Indifferent masses and the implosion of the social." _European Journal of Social Theory_ 29, no. 4 (2026): 721--739. <https://doi.org/10.1177/13684310261429881>.
> *ABSTRACT:* This article examines Jean Baudrillard's overlooked engagement with political resistance. Focusing on his writings in the aftermath of May 1968 through the late 1970s, it reconstructs a trajectory marked by growing scepticism towards political action and critique. Central to this trajectory is Baudrillard's articulation of the disappearance of any viable form of resistance and his introduction of new notions, namely ‘mass’ and ‘implosion’. Particular attention is given to the 1978 silent majorities essay, where he frames the ‘indifference’ of the masses as a mode of resistance, marking the culmination of a decade-long inquiry into its disappearance. While the article reads Baudrillard's anti-critical thought as a bleak diagnostic of the end of thinking resistance, it argues that his work remains relevant as a warning about the ambivalence of indifference as resistance, which can function both as a refusal of power, akin to ‘destituent’ thought, and as a condition of contemporary domination.

3. Clue, Natalie Naomi. "Archiving resistance: Claudia Jones and the racial historiography of marketing in postwar Britain." _Journal of Historical Research in Marketing_ 18, no. 3 (2026): 355--384. <https://doi.org/10.1108/JHRM-09-2025-0061>.
> *ABSTRACT:* This study introduces Claudia Jones (1915–1964) into the canon of marketing history by examining her pioneering use of media, cultural events and symbolic consumption production as strategies of marketplace reclamation and racial counter-narrative in postwar Britain. It aims to explore how Jones’s activism created alternative communicative infrastructures that challenged dominant racialized media and marketplace narratives between 1948 and 1964.Adopting a biographical, socio-historical and historiographical methodology, this study draws on primary archival material housed at The National Archives of the UK, the British Library (London), the Wellcome Collection and the Lambeth Archives (London). Secondary literature in Black British cultural studies, postcolonial historiography and critical marketing scholarship is referenced to contextualize Jones’s cultural praxis work through the West Indian Gazette and the inaugural indoor Caribbean Carnival.This examination reveals how Jones’s communicative strategies, spanning journalism, cultural organizing and public discourse, functioned as powerful interventions into dominant narratives of race and belonging. Through platforms such as the West Indian Gazette and the London Caribbean Carnival, Jones crafted cultural tools that contested the exclusionary logic of British media, government policy and commercial messaging. These acts of symbolic resistance not only reasserted diasporic presence but also forged new pathways for identity formation and collective empowerment within the British marketplace.This study contributes to emerging scholarship that links race, empire and consumption to challenge the dominant Western-centric frameworks of marketing history. By centering a non-US, diasporic figure such as Claudia Jones, the research directly responds to calls for geographically and racially expansive marketing scholarship (Grier et al., 2024). It positions Jones as a cultural strategist whose work complicates conventional conceptualizations of marketing by showing how communication, resistance and community-building can serve as foundational market practices. In doing so, this study offers both a historiographical amplification and a theoretical provocation to reconsider the boundaries of marketing thought.

4. Cuttica, Cesare. "Democratic Caesarism and the cultural critique of the masses in Victorian Britain." _Modern Intellectual History_ 23, no. 3 (2026): 747--766. <https://doi.org/10.1017/S147924432610078X>.
> *ABSTRACT:* This article reverses prevailing scholarly assumptions according to which in Victorian Britain Caesarism was neglected in both theoretical parlance and public discussion. It shows how intellectuals as ideologically different as Bagehot, Seeley, and Maine, as well as other (less well known) commentators writing in newspapers and periodicals, theorized about Caesarism–Napoleonism primarily according to cultural considerations. This means that they looked at “democratic Caesarism” as a form of society rather than exclusively as a political phenomenon, emphasizing how a series of civilizational deficiencies attributed to the masses enabled Caesarist politics. Moreover, the article argues that these critiques were part of a broader (Arnoldian) antidemocratic discourse that targeted democracy as a cultural desert. Democratic Caesarism was thus seen as being characterized by the masses’ uncultured attitudes, ethical–political irresponsibility, and exaggerated care for materialistic interests. The main goal of this criticism was to expose the damaging consequences of democratic life for British politics and civilization.

5. Daros, Otávio. "Mass Communication Research: the “dominant paradigm” that was never dominant in Brazil|Mass communication research: O “paradigma dominante” que nunca foi dominante no Brasil." _MATRIZes_ 20, no. 2 (2026): 97--115. <https://doi.org/10.11606/issn.1982-8160.v20i2p97-115>.
> *ABSTRACT:* It has become common knowledge that Mass Communication Research has consolidated itself as the dominant paradigm in the development of the field of communication, including in Brazil. This article questions this view by examining pioneering Brazilian titles from the 1960s and 1970s. The analysis undertaken deconstructs the idea of a homogenizing US influence, revealing that references from different schools were, from the beginning, equally or even more present. The inclusion of the Brazilian experience in a tradition marked more by eclectic essays than by scientific empiricism provides evidence to question whether the universalizing hegemony of Mass Communication Research does not deserve to be treated as an academic myth.|Virou senso comum a ideia de que a Mass Communication Research (Pesquisa em Comunicação de Massa) se impôs como paradigma dominante na formação do campo da comunicação, inclusive no Brasil. Este artigo problematiza essa visão mediante exame de títulos nacionais precursores, datados das décadas de 1960 e 1970. A análise empreendida desconstrói o ideário de uma influência estadunidense homogeneizante, revelando que, desde cedo, referenciais de diferentes escolas foram tão ou mais presentes. A inscrição da experiência brasileira em uma tradição marcada antes pelo ensaísmo eclético do que pelo empirismo cientificista fornece indícios para questionar se a hegemonia universalizante da Mass Communication Research não merece ser tratada como um mito acadêmico.

6. Earle, Samuel. "Gabriel Tarde’s crowded publics: Rediscovering the work of a forgotten media theorist." _International Journal of Communication_ 20 (2026): 2480--2498. <https://doi.org/10.65476/1khjyc86>.
> *ABSTRACT:* Despite a revival of interest in the work of Gabriel Tarde, he remains an underappreciated, patchily translated, and often misrepresented thinker. When he is invoked, it is typically as the “forefather” of actor–network theory, as claimed by Bruno Latour; as the advocate of the power of conversation in media influence, as promoted by Elihu Katz; or as a useful, but inaccurate, citation for comparing rational publics with irrational crowds. This study aims to correct certain misconceptions and broaden knowledge of Tarde’s work as a media and communications scholar by focusing on two texts, “The Public and the Crowd” (where anglophone readers have typically relied on incomplete translations) and Les Transformations du Pouvoir (which remains untranslated). Tarde teaches us that the media was always social and that the line between physical crowds and mediated audiences was always blurry.

7. Fari, Simone. "Mobilities studies and history: The evolution of a troubled relation." _Mobilities_ 21, no. 5 (2026): 1348--1366. <https://doi.org/10.1080/17450101.2025.2611879>.
> *ABSTRACT:* This article critically examines the evolving relationship between mobilities studies and historical research over the past three decades. Initially rooted in postmodern, presentist frameworks, the mobilities paradigm largely overlooked historical methodologies. From the 2010s onward, however, scholars both within and beyond mobilities studies began to integrate archival sources, life writing, and long-term analyses, giving rise to a distinct subfield of ‘mobilities history’. This evolution unfolded in three phases: (1) an a-historical beginning (1990s–2000s), characterized by a focus on contemporary flows; (2) a growing inclusion of historical perspectives during the 2010s, marked by foundational work in transport history and interdisciplinary collaborations; and (3) the consolidation of historical approaches in the 2020s, which has expanded the field’s geographical scope to include the Global South and East and deepened engagement with decolonial, feminist, and sustainability concerns. Drawing on a systematic literature review, the article highlights key theoretical and methodological contributions, from Sheller and Urry’s foundational texts to recent decolonial critiques. It concludes by discussing the practical implications of historical specificity for contemporary mobility policies, especially in the context of sustainable urban planning and mobility justice.

8. Grier, Sonya A., Guillaume Johnson, and Kevin D. Thomas. "Then, now, next: A brief history of the Race in the Marketplace (RIM) research network." _Journal of Historical Research in Marketing_ 18, no. 3 (2026): 251--263. <https://doi.org/10.1108/JHRM-05-2026-0026>.
> *ABSTRACT:* The purpose of this paper is to provide a timeline and account of the development of the Race in the Marketplace (RIM) research network as it nears its 10th anniversary in 2027. Written by the network’s three co-founders, it complements the Guest Editors’ introduction to the special issue, Historical Perspectives on RIM, in the Journal of Historical Research in Marketing.The RIM co-founders present a firsthand narrative of the organization’s development, highlighting the collaborative efforts that have shaped its inception and growth.The paper presents a visual timeline and narrative highlighting the RIM network’s key accomplishments and academic contributions.In a global political climate where critical research on race is under attack, this piece reaffirms the network’s longstanding intellectual commitment: to advance understanding of race and markets in ways that support social justice.

9. Marchi, Leonardo De. "An unnoticed Media Philosopher: The philosophy of Bernard Stiegler as a media theory|Um despercebido filósofo da mídia: O pensamento de Bernard Stiegler como teoria da mídia." _MATRIZes_ 20, no. 2 (2026): 117--139. <https://doi.org/10.11606/issn.1982-8160.v20i2p117-139>.
> *ABSTRACT:* Bernard Stiegler (1952-2020) is one of the leading contemporary philosophers of technology. There is an important dimension in his oeuvre that has not yet gained due attention among its commentators. It is his thinking about media, understood as technologies of inscription, processing and distribution of information. Disciple of Jacques Derrida, Stiegler would deepen the theory of the writing (l’écriture), transforming it into a theory of media materiality. The article argues that the basis of Bernard Stiegler’s thoughts lies in a media philosophy. The objective of this effort is to highlight the possible contributions of the author to media studies.| Bernard Stiegler (1952-2020) é considerado um dos principais filósofos contemporâneos da técnica. Há uma importante dimensão em sua obra que, contudo, ainda não ganhou a devida atenção entre seus comentadores. Trata-se de seu pensamento sobre mídia, entendida como tecnologias de inscrição, processamento e distribuição de informação. Discípulo de Jacques Derrida, Stiegler aprofundaria a teoria da escritura (l’écriture), transformando-a em uma teoria da materialidade da mídia. Neste artigo, defende-se que a base do pensamento de Bernard Stiegler reside em uma verdadeira filosofia da mídia. A partir da leitura selecionada de seus livros, apresenta-se um panorama de suas reflexões, advogando que elas estão assentadas sobre uma teoria da mídia. O objetivo deste esforço é destacar as possíveis contribuições do autor aos estudos de mídia

10. Mendoza-Canales, Ricardo. "Spirituality in Simondon’s theory of individuation: Affect, signification, and the transindividual." _Theory, Culture & Society_ 43, no. 6 (2026): 83--101. <https://doi.org/10.1177/02632764261460854>.
> *ABSTRACT:* This article offers a systematic reconstruction of the under-examined yet central role of spirituality in Gilbert Simondon’s theory of individuation. It argues that spirituality functions as the key operator that resolves the disparity between the psychic and the collective, thereby opening the domain of the transindividual. The analysis first revisits Simondon’s ontogenetic process to highlight the role of disparity and signification in the phase-shifts that lead from the pre-individual to the transindividual. It then shows how spirituality – understood as signification – emerges from the pre-individual charge carried by each being, binding the subject to the collective. Crucially, this spirituality is not metaphysical but operational: it constitutes a non-religious ‘eternity’ in which signification is deposited in technical objects and cultural forms for future reactualization. Ultimately, the article contends that spirituality names the transductive convergence of emotion and action, grounding an ethics of disparity in the metastable and open-ended process of collective individuation.

11. Mercea, Dan. "Editorial comment: Looking back to go forward." _Information, Communication & Society_ 29, no. 13 (2026): 3825--3835. <https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2025.2565322>.
> *ABSTRACT:* [first paragraph, in lieu of abstract] In March 2024, after editing Information, Communication & Society (iCS) for 26 years, Brian Loader passed on the baton to a new team. Siân Brooke, Maria Kyriakidou, Michael Saker, Victor Ávila Torres and Stefania Vicari are the members of the new editorial team that I now lead. This editorial comment is a reflective pause after a busy year of us settling into our roles. In the year to March 2025, iCS received 1,669 submissions. From them and after excluding submissions to special issues (n = 61) and book reviews (n = 53), we selected 36 per cent for peer review. While only a single figure, when set side-by-side with the number of submissions to other journals in our intellectual neighbourhood (see Jones, 2024), the number of submissions tells the story of a vibrant field of studies. Likewise, it provides an opportunity for at least three observations. First, that a good number of authors value the journal enough to want to have their scholarship published in it. Second, that my colleagues have committed a very substantial amount of time and effort to the review process – to finding reviewers (in that year, four in five invitations to review were turned down) and collaborating with them for us to be able to reach informed and, in as much as possible, judicious decisions. Third, that referees who lend their time to reviewing for the journal make a remarkable contribution to maintaining the quality of the scholarship we publish; and they do so, likely, alongside many other commitments. Therefore, I am immensely grateful to both authors and reviewers and feel privileged to be able to work with them and with my colleagues to publish innovative, rigorous, and critical research. To continue to do so, I have looked back, to take stock of the journey the journal has been on, to date; and look forward, to imagine how that journey may progress.

12. Moore, Ryan. "Hegemony, resistance, and the cultural turn: Revisiting the Birmingham School of Cultural Studies." _Critical Sociology_ 52, no. 7-8 (2026): 1635--1651. <https://doi.org/10.1177/08969205251361448>.
> *ABSTRACT:* The theoretical innovations and empirical research of the Contemporary Centre for Cultural Studies in the 1970s, commonly known as the Birmingham School, are reconsidered as a critical sociological approach to culture. Led by Stuart Hall, Birmingham School scholars theorized that the social crisis of their time was ultimately determined by shifts in the political economy of capitalism, but their ethnographic research also illuminated multiple sites of opposition and resistance among subcultures involving working class and nonclass subjectivities. Their perspective on class and culture thus exemplifies the class dynamism approach which opposes the class abstractionism that has characterized some Marxist thought in recent years. The Birmingham School is also contrasted with the forms of U.S.-based cultural sociology which theorize culture from a Durkheimian-Parsonian standpoint as an autonomous symbolic order. The early cultural studies of the CCCS therefore represents a vital alternative to both class abstractionism and mainstream cultural sociology. They developed a kind of Marxist sociology that can dialectically integrate insights from the ‘cultural turn’, which is conceptualized not simply as an intellectual trend but as part of a socio-historical condition created by late capitalism.

13. Navarro, Raúl Fuentes, Maria Immacolata Vassallo de Lopes, and Ana Carolina Escosteguy. "Honoring Rossana Reguillo (1955-2026) and recognizing her legacy Authors|Homenageando Rossana Reguillo (1955-2026) e reconhecendo seu legado." _MATRIZes_ 20, no. 2 (2026): 7--16. <https://doi.org/10.11606/issn.1982-8160.v20i2p7-16>.
> *ABSTRACT:* The Scientific Editorial Committee of MATRIZes called upon the international communication and culture research community to collaborate in a collective academic tribute to Rossana Reguillo, whose passing on April 25, 2026, represents a regrettable loss, but also constitutes a testament to the transcendence of her work. As part of this recognition, MATRIZes is publishing a collection of mostly unpublished short signed messages as a mosaic of expressions of affection and appreciation for Rossana’s important contribution to the understanding of communication and culture in contemporary societies.|O Comitê Científico Editorial da MATRIZes convocou a comunidade internacional de pesquisa em comunicação e cultura para colaborar em uma homenagem acadêmica coletiva a Rossana Reguillo, cujo falecimento, em 25 de abril de 2026, representa uma perda lamentável, mas também constitui um testemunho da transcendência de seu trabalho. Como parte desse reconhecimento, a revista publica uma coletânea de breves mensagens assinadas, a maioria delas inéditas, como um mosaico de expressões de carinho e reconhecimento pela importante contribuição de Rossana ao conhecimento sobre comunicação e cultura nas sociedades contemporâneas.

14. Pittel, Harald. "Raymond Williams and the performativity of the ‘common dialectic’." _new formations: a journal of culture/theory/politics_ 115, no. 115 (2026): 76--91. <https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/248/article/1003236>.
> *ABSTRACT:* This essay argues that present-day discussions about the commons
can gain from Raymond Williams’ thought. Specifically, Williams
envisioned a dialectical relation between ‘common culture’ (as an
open and participatory process) and ‘culture in common’ (cultural
forms in which the process imperfectly crystallises and from which
it restarts). I argue that this way of theorising the commons –
which I suggest calling Williams’ ‘common dialectic’ – is
consistent in his work. A case in point is Williams’ view of
working-class writing transforming established forms of literature
(a cultural possession held ‘in common’) in a creative process that
is inherently ‘common’, open and democratic. Even though the later
Williams tended to dismiss the terms constituting his ‘common
dialectic’, it can be argued that this way of thinking shifted to
an implicit and performative mode and kept informing his commitment
as a public intellectual.

15. Rich, Aaron. "Library science in research departments and the systemization of visual knowledge in early Hollywood." _Film History: An International Journal_ 36, no. 4 (2026): 119--141. <https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/3/article/1004767>.
> *ABSTRACT:* Early Hollywood film production companies systematized visual knowledge by adapting library science methods to create research departments that supported their workers. From 1913 to 1928, studio researchers, who were predominantly women, collaborated with public librarians and one another to collect, organize, and index illustrated materials to serve as guideline materials for filmmakers. These research libraries functioned as crucial but overlooked reference desks serving the film community, helping craft departments find visual sources to create believable details and settings for movies, thereby transforming cinema from pure entertainment into a conveyor of useful information for global audiences.

16. Sun, Ningxiang. "Towards an ecology of time: Revisiting Simondon’s genetic method." _Theory, Culture & Society_ 43, no. 6 (2026): 59--82. <https://doi.org/10.1177/02632764251407520>.
> *ABSTRACT:* This article explores the methodological dimension of Gilbert Simondon’s technical thought, focusing on the ‘genetic method’ he introduced to analyse the evolution of technical objects. While holding significant promise, this approach has been largely underutilized, a fact that can be attributed to its perceived tensions with dominant social constructivist perspectives and even with his own theory of individuation. To address these tensions, this article situates Simondon’s method within a broader intellectual context and weaves together his theory of evolution and invention with insights from George Kubler and Alfred Gell on the succession of artifacts, thereby developing an ‘ecology of time’. This framework, characterized by both its operational viability for empirical inquiry and its fidelity to Simondon’s original project, holds the potential to render his profound insights more accessible to a wider scholarly audience and to catalyse new reverberations between his technical thought and our contemporary technical realities.

17. Yu, Kaidong, Jose Ferreira Pinto, and José C. Alves. "Mobilizing meanings: The scholarly legacy of Kevin Hannam." _Mobilities_ 21, no. 5 (2026): 1367--1378. <https://doi.org/10.1080/17450101.2026.2619593>.
> *ABSTRACT:* This paper serves both as a tribute to the late Professor Kevin Hannam and as a comprehensive review of his scholarly contributions to tourism and mobilities research. Hannam was a central figure in shaping the ‘mobil- ities turn’, and his work significantly expanded the theoretical and empirical horizons of tourism studies. Drawing on an in-depth analysis of his eleven books and over sixty peer-reviewed articles, this paper identifies three core themes that characterize his intellectual legacy: performing mobilities, decolonizing tourism, and applying mobilities theory in practice. These themes reflect his interdisciplinary orientation, global collaborations, and sustained attention to power, identity, and ethics in mobile worlds. Beyond synthesis, the paper also proposes a future research agenda inspired by Hannam’s work, offering guiding questions for continued exploration in tourism and mobilities. By combining scholarly reflection with commemo- rative intent, the paper honours Hannam’s enduring influence while pro- viding a structured resource for future research.
